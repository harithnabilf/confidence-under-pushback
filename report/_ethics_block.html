Appendix D  Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

What the results do and do not establish. They show how four open-weight models, under one fixed and argument-free pushback protocol on multiple-choice questions, relate their verbalized confidence to what they defend. They do not show how these models behave in open-ended advice, with real users, with evidence-bearing arguments, or in other languages. They also say nothing directly about closed frontier models, which we could not test at zero cost.

Methodological limitations. (1) Single sample, single phrasing. We use temperature 0, one fixed ladder and one prompt format. The turn-by-turn curves therefore conflate the escalation type (doubt → textbook → authority) with plain repetition. (2) Implausible targets. C2W and W2W push toward a uniformly random wrong option, which is often obviously wrong. Pushing toward the most tempting distractor would likely raise capitulation, so our C2W rates are best read as lower bounds. (3) Small cells. W2C and W2W sizes depend on how many items each model gets wrong, so their CIs are wide. Qwen3.8-27B, the slowest model under the free-tier token limit, completed fewest items. (4) Contamination. MMLU-Pro is public, which may inflate both accuracy and resistance. (5) Normative model. The threshold rule assumes uniform residual credence and symmetric user errors, and treats verbalized confidence as the model's operative credence. r* is a descriptive summary of behaviour, not a claim that the models perform Bayesian inference. It is uninformative when a model adopts (or resists) at every confidence level, so we report it alongside the turn-level AUROC. (6) Scoring choices. We stop a ladder once the model adopts the target, so we do not measure later flip-backs. Unparseable answers count as "not adopted", which slightly lowers adoption rates. We also keep only items under 650 characters.

Dual-use. The pushback ladder is a mild social-engineering script. That models can be talked out of correct answers is already well documented [1, 2, 3], and we release no new attack. Our measurements could in principle help someone select an easily swayed model or tune persuasion against one. We judge this risk small relative to the value of making the gap measurable. A second risk is Goodharting. A model trained only to minimise capitulation would learn stubbornness, which would also destroy correction. We therefore always report capitulation together with correction and the W2W control, and argue that the right target is confidence-consistent deference, not resistance.

Personal data and cost. No human subjects or personal data were involved. The inputs are public benchmark questions, and the outputs are model generations, all of which are released. All model calls used free-tier access, at zero monetary cost.
