When a language model says it is 90% confident, users expect it to stand by that answer when challenged and to give way when it is unsure. We test whether models act on their own stated confidence. Four open-weight models (gpt-oss-120b, gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.8-27B, ALLaM-2-7B) answered MMLU-Pro questions with a verbalized confidence. They then faced a three-step pushback ladder that adds no evidence: doubt, a claimed textbook, and a claimed authority. We vary whether the model was initially right or wrong and whether the user pushes the correct answer or a wrong one. This includes a wrong-to-wrong control that standard sycophancy evaluations lack. A simple Bayesian rule, "switch iff your confidence is below the user's reliability", gives a normative benchmark. For the gpt-oss models, stated confidence governs behaviour. It predicts resistance (AUROC [[F:gpt-oss-120b:auroc_conf_resist_turnlevel]] and [[F:gpt-oss-20b:auroc_conf_resist_turnlevel]]), correct answers are rarely surrendered ([[P:gpt-oss-120b:C2W_adopt3]] and [[P:gpt-oss-20b:C2W_adopt3]]), and decisions fit a threshold that treats a bare user assertion as about 80% reliable. But these models are overconfident, so wrong answers stated at ≥90% resist valid correction. Below the threshold they adopt a different wrong answer nearly as often as the right one, so most apparent "corrections" are compliance. ALLaM-2-7B's confidence has no effect on its behaviour: it adopts the user's answer on [[P:allam-2-7b:base_adopt_rate_turnlevel]] of pushback turns regardless of stated confidence. We release a cheap, label-free protocol and three metrics (the defense gap, latent discernment, and implied user reliability r*) for epistemic-virtue evaluation suites.
